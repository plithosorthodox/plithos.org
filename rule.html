On the Prayer Rule

What the Church's sources say about how much to pray, and about praying according to what one is able. This page reports those sources; it does not add counsel to them.

Read this first. Everything on Plithos is a reference, not a measure of you. The prayers, the fasting rule, the hours and the orders of service are set out here in full because they are the Church's inheritance and should be available to everyone. Setting them out in full is not the same as saying that anyone must keep all of it.

The rule you keep is set by your spiritual father, according to your knowledge, your capability, and your circumstances. Nothing on this site overrides that, and nothing here is a judgement on anyone who prays less than what is printed. A person who says the Lord's Prayer three times a day and means it is keeping the rule the Church gave in the first century.

This page reports what the sources say and does not add counsel of its own. Where a sentence carries no citation it is a statement about this website, not direction about your prayer. Direction belongs to your priest, who knows your life; this site does not.

What the earliest rule actually was

The oldest surviving Christian manual of practice is the Didache, from around the year 100. Its rule of prayer is three Our Fathers a day.

Thrice in the day thus pray.

The Didache 8 · tr. M. B. Riddle, 1886 · read in the Library

The same document, two chapters earlier, states the principle of measure directly, and it is not a modern softening but the earliest thing the Church wrote down on the subject:

For if you are able to bear all the yoke of the Lord, you will be perfect; but if you are not able, what you are able that do.

The Didache 6 · tr. M. B. Riddle, 1886 · read in the Library

The fuller orders of prayer in later prayer books developed after this. The Didache is simply the earliest written rule the Church has, and this is what it requires.

Quantity is not the measure

St John Cassian, recording the teaching of the Egyptian fathers in the early fifth century, warns against reckoning prayer by the times one formally sets aside for it:

For he prays too little, who is accustomed only to pray at the times when he bends his knees.

St John Cassian, The Conferences 10.14 · tr. Edgar C. S. Gibson · read in the Library

Cassian's measure of prayer is therefore wider than the appointed times, and the sentence is directed at those who confine it to them. He is not introducing the idea. The Apostle had already given the rule in a form no clock can hold:

Praying always with all prayer and supplication in the Spirit, and watching thereunto with all perseverance and supplication for all saints;

Ephesians 6:18, Authorised Version

And more briefly still: Pray without ceasing (1 Thessalonians 5:17). A rule of set hours serves that; it does not exhaust it.

An ordering, not a ladder

What follows is not Plithos prescribing tiers. It is an ordering of what the sources and the received books contain, by how much of a day each one asks for. Which of these applies to a given person is a question for their spiritual father, not for this page.

It is not a ladder, and the first entry is the reason it is not. The Jesus Prayer stands first because it asks least of a person's circumstances, not because it asks least of the person. The same prayer is what the hesychast fathers describe as the whole of prayer, and what those furthest along the way are given in place of everything written. It stands at both ends at once.

The Jesus Prayer

When there is no time, no book, and no quiet - travelling, at work, ill, unable to stand or to speak aloud

The Jesus Prayer in its full form: Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner. Where even that is too much, the Fathers permit shorter forms - Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me, or simply Lord, have mercy. It needs no book, no standing, and no voice, and it can be carried through an ordinary working day.

  • The Jesus Prayer, and the counsel of the Fathers on keeping it

It comes first because it survives every circumstance, not because it is the least of what is here. Cassian, Conferences 10.14: "he prays too little, who is accustomed only to pray at the times when he bends his knees."

The Lord's Prayer, three times in the day

A fixed rule at set times, when one is possible

The rule of the earliest Christian manual we possess. It is a small rule, but it is a rule at fixed hours, and so asks more of a broken day than the Jesus Prayer does.

The Didache 8.

The rule of St Seraphim of Sarov

For those whose work leaves no room for a longer rule

On rising, standing before the icons: the Lord's Prayer three times, O Virgin Theotokos, rejoice three times, and the Creed once. Then to one's work, saying the Jesus Prayer quietly through the morning, and Most Holy Theotokos, save me through the afternoon. The same short rule again before sleep.

St Seraphim gave it to labourers and householders who could not keep the fuller morning and evening prayers, and he did not treat it as a concession to be grown out of. It is a rule of fixed prayers at fixed times joined to a prayer that runs through the working day, which is why it sits here rather than lower down.

St Seraphim of Sarov, in his instructions to laypeople. The prayers themselves are on the Prayers page.

Morning and evening

The customary rule of the prayer book for a layman

The morning prayers on rising and the evening prayers before sleep, said with attention rather than hurried through.

Before receiving the Mysteries

The Order of Preparation, the two prayers said at the chalice, and the thanksgiving after. How much of the Order is said, and whether it is kept the evening before or the morning of, differs by tradition and is set by one's spiritual father. When you cannot keep your rule

Travel, illness, night work, small children, grief and exhaustion all interrupt a rule. Two things the texts above bear directly on that.

The Didache does not present the rule as all or nothing. Its instruction is not to keep the whole yoke or else nothing, but that where the whole cannot be borne, "what you are able that do".

Prayer is not confined to the times of standing. This is the force of Cassian's sentence above: he reproaches those who reckon their prayer only by the times they bend the knee, which places prayer outside those times within the reckoning rather than outside it. The Jesus Prayer in its shorter received forms requires neither book, posture, nor voice.

What the Fathers make the condition of approaching

The Order of Preparation is a real good and a real inheritance. It is not what makes a person able to receive. On this the Fathers are direct, and they were writing long before any printed order of preparation existed.

St John Chrysostom, preaching on Hebrews, takes up exactly the question of how often and how much, and refuses to answer it in those terms:

What then? Which shall we approve? Those [who receive] once [in the year]? Those who [receive] many times? Those who [receive] few times? Neither those [who receive] once, nor those [who receive] often, nor those [who receive] seldom, but those [who come] with a pure conscience, from a pure heart, with an irreproachable life. Let such draw near continually; but those who are not such, not even once.

St John Chrysostom, Homily 17 on Hebrews · tr. Frederic Gardiner · read in the Library

The criterion Chrysostom names is the state of the conscience. He does not name a number, either of communions or of prayers said beforehand, and he sets aside the question of frequency as the wrong one to be asking.

St John Cassian addresses the other half of it, and the chapter heading says the thing plainly: that although we acknowledge that we cannot be without sin, yet still we ought not to suspend ourselves from the Lord's Communion.

Yet we ought not to suspend ourselves from the Lord's Communion because we confess ourselves sinners, but should more and more eagerly hasten to it for the healing of our soul, and purifying of our spirit, and seek the rather a remedy for our wounds with humility of mind and faith, as considering ourselves unworthy to receive so great grace.

Otherwise we cannot worthily receive the Communion even once a year, as some do, who live in monasteries and so regard the dignity and holiness and value of the heavenly sacraments, as to think that none but saints and spotless persons should venture to receive them, and not rather that they would make us saints and pure by taking them. And these thereby fall into greater presumption and arrogance than what they seem to themselves to avoid, because at the time when they do receive them, they consider that they are worthy to receive them.

St John Cassian, Conferences 23.21 · tr. Edgar C. S. Gibson · read in the Library

Cassian's argument is that abstaining from a sense of unworthiness is itself the greater presumption, since the one who abstains until he is worthy supposes that on some other day he would be. Against that he sets what he says the Mysteries are for: not a reward for those already pure, but the means by which, in his words, they would make us saints and pure by taking them.

What the Church has a communicant say about himself

This is not a lowering of the standard. It is the standard, and the Church puts it in the mouth of everyone who approaches. The confession said at the chalice, aloud, by the whole congregation, does not say that the one saying it has arrived:

I believe, O Lord, and I confess that Thou art truly the Christ, the Son of the living God, Who camest into the world to save sinners, of whom I am first.

The prayer before receiving · after 1 Timothy 1:15 · read in the prayer book

The Apostle's own words are Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners; of whom I am chief, and the Church did not leave them in the epistle. She put them at the chalice and gave them to every communicant to say in the first person, on the day he receives. The saints are those who said that sentence and meant it, not those who reached a point where it stopped applying. Grace is given; what is asked of us is that we answer, keep the commandments we are given, and go on answering after we have failed to keep them.

What this means in practice

The Order is not among the canonical conditions. No canon of the Church requires it before receiving, and the fixed printed sequence is later than the canons; how much of it is said is set by one's spiritual father.

What the tradition does name before the chalice is a conscience not resting in unrepented sin, peace with one's neighbour, the eucharistic fast, and obedience to one's spiritual father on confession. Cassian addresses the case of the person who has abstained on the ground of unworthiness directly, in the passage above; Chrysostom addresses the person reckoning by counts.

The eucharistic fast is canonical, not customary. The African Code required it, allowing one exception in the year, on the day the Lord's Supper is commemorated:

That the Sacraments of the Altar are not to be celebrated except by those who are fasting, except on the one anniversary of the celebration of the Lord's Supper; for if the commemoration of some of the dead, whether bishops or others, is to be made in the afternoon, let it be only with prayers, if those who officiate have already breakfasted.

The African Code (419), Canon 41 · tr. Henry R. Percival, 1900 · read in the Library

The Council in Trullo took up that canon and removed the exception:

A canon of the Synod of Carthage says that the holy mysteries of the altar are not to be performed but by men who are fasting, except on one day in the year on which the Supper of the Lord is celebrated. At that time, on account perhaps of certain occasions in those places useful to the Church, even the holy Fathers themselves made use of this dispensation. But since nothing leads us to abandon exact observance, we decree that the Apostolic and Patristic tradition shall be followed; and define that it is not right to break the fast on the fifth feria of the last week of Lent, and thus to do dishonour to the whole of Lent.

The Council in Trullo, the Quinisext (692), Canon 29 · tr. Henry R. Percival, 1900 · read in the Library

What the canons require is the fast. Reckoning it from the previous midnight is the custom that carries it, and it is the ordinary practice in every jurisdiction. Where the Liturgy is served in the evening, as the Presanctified is and as the Liturgies of Nativity, Theophany, Holy Thursday and Holy Saturday can be, the period is set by the bishop rather than run from midnight. Medicine that must be taken, illness, pregnancy, infancy and great age are met by one's spiritual father. Asking is not the same as being excused, and neither is a reason to stay away from the chalice.

The two prayers said at the chalice - "I believe, O Lord, and I confess" and "Of Thy Mystical Supper" - are said aloud with the whole congregation in most parishes.

The rules are monastic in form; the life they ask for is not

Almost every fixed rule printed in a prayer book reached its present shape in a monastery, and that is why preparation can read as though it were addressed to somebody else. The objection is old enough that St John Chrysostom puts it in his hearers' mouths before answering it:

I am not, you will say, one of the monks, but I have both a wife and children, and the care of a household. Why, this is what has ruined all, your supposing that the reading of the divine Scriptures appertains to those only, when you need it much more than they. For they that dwell in the world, and each day receive wounds, these have most need of medicines.

St John Chrysostom, Homily 2 on Matthew · tr. George Prevost · read in the Library

He makes the same point about the way of life itself, and he makes it as a refusal to legislate the desert:

I do not make it a law that you are to occupy the mountains and the deserts, but to be good and considerate and chaste, dwelling in the midst of the city.

St John Chrysostom, Homily 7 on Matthew · tr. George Prevost · read in the Library

And on Romans he treats the idea that the ordinary life is the wrong address for the commandments as an accusation against the faith rather than a description of it:

For if there is no possibility for a person who is living in the midst of cities to be a disciple, this is a sad imputation on this rule of conduct, that we are to leave the cities, and run to the deserts. But show me a man who has a wife, and children, and family, and yet pursues wisdom. What are we then to say to all this? Must we not hang down our heads, and be ashamed? For Christ gave us no such commandment; but what? Let your light shine before men, not mountains, and deserts, and wildernesses, and out-of-the-way places.

St John Chrysostom, Homily 26 on Romans · tr. J. B. Morris and W. H. Simcox · read in the Library

He is careful, in the sentence that follows, not to be read as disparaging the monastics: he says he speaks "not as abusing those who have taken up with the mountains, but as bewailing those that dwell in cities, because they have banished virtue from thence." The monastic gives up his own will to an elder. The layman is given the same thing to give up, in a marriage, in work he did not choose, in the patience a household costs, and it is asked of him in a form no rule can print in advance.

Fasting, confession, and the rest of the preparation

Preparation is not only prayer. The Wednesday and Friday fast is the oldest part of it, and it is in the same chapter of the Didache that gives the rule of prayer:

But let not your fasts be with the hypocrites; for they fast on the second and fifth day of the week; but fast on the fourth day and the Preparation (Friday). Neither pray as the hypocrites; but as the Lord commanded in His Gospel, thus pray: Our Father who art in heaven...

The Didache 8 · tr. M. B. Riddle, 1886 · read in the Library

The warning in the passage attaches to both halves: not to failing to fast but to fasting as the hypocrites, and not to failing to pray but to praying as the hypocrites. The Didache gives the two rules in one breath and qualifies each in the same way.

Confession before communion is expected in some traditions and not in others, and the difference is long-standing rather than a matter of laxity. It is worth setting out, because someone who has learned to prepare in one tradition and then finds himself in a parish of the other can easily take a difference of custom for a judgement on him.

In the Slavic churches - Russian, ROCOR, the OCA, Ukrainian, Serbian - confession is commonly expected before each communion. The preparation customarily includes the three canons, to the Saviour, to the Theotokos and to the Guardian Angel, and often the Akathist to the Sweetest Lord Jesus, in addition to the prayers of preparation; and presence at the vigil or vespers of the evening before is expected as part of preparing rather than as a separate devotion.

In Greek, Antiochian and Romanian practice confession is more often separated from communion and kept at intervals set by one's spiritual father, frequently in the four fasting seasons. Preparation more often means the prayers of preparation rather than the full suite of canons and akathist, and the evening service is strongly encouraged without being made a condition of receiving.

Both are the settled practice of whole local churches, and each is kept under the direction of that church's clergy. A person's rule here comes from his own spiritual father, not from whichever description he happens to read first.

A closing word on measure

A rule exists so that we neither give in to laziness on the one hand nor drive ourselves past our strength on the other. St Theophan the Recluse taught that the path has two ditches: to the right, zeal without knowledge; to the left, sloth. Excess brings the beginner to exhaustion and despair when he fails, and to pride when he succeeds. St Ignatius Brianchaninov wrote that the rule exists for you; you do not exist for the rule, but live for the Lord, and must keep the freedom of discernment.

Begin small. It is better to say a few prayers with attention than to hurry through many. If you have a spiritual father, keep the rule he gives and change nothing without his blessing; if you do not yet have one, keep a modest rule suited to your strength and seek such a father. No book and no page can take the place of a living father in Christ.

Drawn from St Theophan the Recluse, A Prayer Rule, and St Ignatius (Brianchaninov) on the prayer rule · also in the Guide
