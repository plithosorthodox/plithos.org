On the Prayer Rule

What the Church's sources actually say about how much to pray, and about praying according to what you are able.

Read this first. Everything on Plithos is a reference, not a measure of you. The prayers, the fasting rule, the hours and the orders of service are set out here in full because they are the Church's inheritance and should be available to everyone. Setting them out in full is not the same as saying that anyone must keep all of it.

The rule you keep is set by your spiritual father, according to your knowledge, your capability, and your circumstances. Nothing on this site overrides that, and nothing here is a judgement on anyone who prays less than what is printed. A person who says the Lord's Prayer three times a day and means it is keeping the rule the Church gave in the first century.

If what you find here weighs on you rather than helps you, that is a sign to pray less and more attentively, not more and more anxiously - and to speak with your priest. This site cannot know your life. He can.

What the earliest rule actually was

The oldest surviving Christian manual of practice is the Didache, from around the year 100. Its rule of prayer is three Our Fathers a day.

Thrice in the day thus pray.

The Didache 8 · tr. M. B. Riddle, 1886 · read in the Library

The same document, two chapters earlier, states the principle of measure directly, and it is not a modern softening but the earliest thing the Church wrote down on the subject:

For if you are able to bear all the yoke of the Lord, you will be perfect; but if you are not able, what you are able that do. And concerning food, bear what you are able.

The Didache 6 · tr. M. B. Riddle, 1886 · read in the Library

This is worth holding on to when a prayer book runs to many pages. The fuller rules that developed later are a genuine inheritance and a real good. They are not the floor. The floor is much lower, and it was set deliberately low, in writing, by the generation after the Apostles.

Quantity is not the measure

St John Cassian, recording the teaching of the Egyptian fathers in the early fifth century, warns against reckoning prayer by the times one formally sets aside for it:

For he prays too little, who is accustomed only to pray at the times when he bends his knees.

St John Cassian, The Conferences 10.14 · tr. Edgar C. S. Gibson · read in the Library

The point cuts both ways. It is a rebuke to praying only at appointed times - and it is also a relief, because it means the rule is not the whole of prayer, and a day in which the rule was cut short is not a day without prayer.

A ladder, not a standard

What follows is not Plithos prescribing tiers. It is an ordering of what the sources and the received books actually contain, from least to most, so that a person can find where they are and what the next step might be. Which of these is right for you is a question for your spiritual father, not for this page.

The apostolic minimum

When almost nothing is possible

The Lord's Prayer, three times in the day.

The Didache 8. This is a rule of the Church, not a concession invented for the modern reader.

The Jesus Prayer alone

Travelling, ill, at work, unable to stand or to speak aloud

The Jesus Prayer, said inwardly, at whatever number is possible. It requires no book, no standing, and no voice.

  • The Jesus Prayer and the fathers' counsel on it

Cassian, Conferences 10.14, on prayer not confined to the times of bending the knee. The counsel already published on the Prayers page follows St Ignatius Brianchaninov and St Theophan the Recluse.

Morning and evening

The customary rule of the prayer book for a layman

The morning prayers on rising and the evening prayers before sleep, said with attention rather than hurried through.

  • Morning and Evening prayers

St Theophan the Recluse held the morning and evening prayers of the prayer book to be entirely sufficient for a layman who is beginning. See the note on the Prayers page for the fuller counsel.

Preparation for Communion

Before receiving the Mysteries

The Order of Preparation, the two prayers said at the chalice, and the thanksgiving after. Custom differs on whether the Order is kept the evening before or the morning of, and on how much of it is said.

The extent is set by one's spiritual father. Practice differs markedly between the Slavic and the Greek traditions, both in the preparation required and in how often the faithful commune.

When you cannot keep your rule

Travel, illness, night shifts, small children, grief, and ordinary exhaustion all interrupt a rule. The tradition does not treat this as failure. Two things are worth saying plainly, and both rest on the texts above.

Shorten rather than abandon. A rule cut down to what is possible is still a rule. The Didache's instruction is not "keep the whole yoke or nothing", but "what you are able, that do".

Prayer is not confined to the times of standing. If you cannot stand, pray sitting or lying. If you cannot speak aloud, pray inwardly. If you cannot hold the words, hold one: Lord, have mercy. Cassian's warning is that formal times are too small a measure of prayer, not that prayer outside them does not count.

Still to be added

The prayers kept the evening before Communion - the Canon of Repentance to our Lord Jesus Christ, the Canon to the Theotokos, and the Canon to the Guardian Angel - are not yet in the corpus. Until they are, the evening-before phase on the Prayers page is empty because we lack the texts, not because nothing belongs there.

Confession and communion frequency differ substantially between the Slavic and the Greek and Romanian traditions. That will be set out here as parallel practice with sources, without adjudicating between them.

Further counsel from St Theophan the Recluse, St Ignatius Brianchaninov, the Optina elders and St Basil's Rules will be added as each can be quoted from a text whose translation and provenance can be cited exactly. Nothing will be attributed to a father from memory.

A closing word on measure

The purpose of a rule is to keep prayer from being governed by mood - so that we neither give in to laziness nor drive ourselves past our strength. Both ditches are real, and the second is not the safer one. Zeal beyond one's strength ends in exhaustion and despair when it fails, and in vanity when it succeeds.

Nothing on this site is a scale you are weighed on. It is a library and a calendar. Use what helps you, under obedience, and leave the rest without anxiety.
