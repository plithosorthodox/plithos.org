# Portuguese: the register, settled before the writing starts

Portuguese is the eleventh language and the first Romance language here that
comes to the Saints page with nothing already standing. Spanish had its
1,456 lives written and could read its register off them; French had the
same; Italian had a beginning. Portuguese has no lives, no calendar entries
and no vocabulary, so the register cannot be read off this repository at
all. It has to be settled from the ear of the books the Church actually
prints in Portuguese - the Antiochian and the Russian parishes of Brazil and
Portugal publish a great deal, and the Metropolis of Rio de Janeiro, the
Archdiocese of São Paulo and the Portuguese-language service books of the
Russian Church Abroad are where the words come from.

That is the whole difficulty of this language, and it is worth naming
plainly before the first line: **Portuguese has a complete, fluent,
beautiful Christian vocabulary already, and almost none of it belongs to
this Church.** Five centuries of Latin Christianity in Portugal and in
Brazil put a Roman word within reach of every phrase in this index, and each
one arrives sounding perfectly natural. *Assunção*, *Missa*, *Doutor da
Igreja*, *beato*, *Nossa Senhora*, *padroeiro*, *convento*, *pároco*,
*imagem*, *invenção das relíquias*, *Quaresma* for any fast at all. Not one
of them is a mistranslation. Every one of them is another Church speaking.

## Which Portuguese

**Brazilian Portuguese, in the orthography of the 1990 Agreement.** This is
one decision and it touches thousands of lines, so it is made once here.
Brazil holds by a wide margin the largest Portuguese-speaking Orthodox
population, and the Antiochian and Russian jurisdictions there print more in
Portuguese than anyone else. So: *Antônio* and not *António*, *Nicomédia*,
*Calcedônia*, *Tessalônica*, *Estêvão*, *gênero*, *ícone*. The 1990
orthography is what both countries now write officially, so the trema is
gone (*consequência*) and the hyphen follows the current rule.

Where Brazil and Portugal genuinely differ in the word and not the spelling,
the word a Portuguese reader would also recognise is preferred, because the
page is read on both sides of the Atlantic.

## What Portuguese is not

- **Dormição**, never *Assunção*. The Assumption is a Roman dogma of 1950
  and a different claim about a different thing; the Dormition is a falling
  asleep. *A Dormição da Mãe de Deus*, *o jejum da Dormição*.
- **Divina Liturgia**, never *Missa*. The *Missa* is the Western rite and
  the word carries it.
- **Padre da Igreja**, never *Doutor da Igreja*. The Doctors are a Roman
  category with a list and a process, and the Fathers are not on it.
- **Ícone**, masculine - *o ícone* - never *imagem*, never *estampa*, never
  *quadro*. Where the English says the icon is written, Portuguese may say
  *escrito* or *pintado*; the site says *o ícone o mostra*.
- **Theotokos** where the title is the Council's word, and **Mãe de Deus**
  where the language would simply say it. *Nossa Senhora* is the devotional
  register of another Church and does not stand as an honorific here;
  *Virgem Maria* alone does not either. *Santíssima Mãe de Deus*,
  *sempre-virgem*.
- **Venerável** for the monastic, never *beato*. *Beato* renders the Roman
  *beatus*, a degree one step below canonisation in a process this Church
  does not have. In Orthodox Portuguese **bem-aventurado** is the word for
  the fools for Christ and for the blessed departed, and it is a
  description, not a rank in a process.
- **Jejum** and the named fasts - *a Grande Quaresma*, *o jejum dos
  Apóstolos*, *o jejum da Dormição*, *o jejum da Natividade*. The bare word
  *Quaresma* means the Great Fast and nothing else, and a Nativity fast
  called an Advent is another calendar.
- **Relíquias**, never *restos*, never *despojos*. **Trasladação** for the
  translation of relics and **descoberta** for their uncovering, which are
  two distinct feasts the calendar keeps on two different days.
  *Invenção* is the Latin *inventio* wearing Portuguese, it is the Roman
  calendar's word, and it blurs the two into one.
- **Presbítero** or **sacerdote**, never *pároco*, which is an office in a
  parish register, and never *pastor*.
- **Mosteiro** and **lavra**; a *convento* is a house of a Western order and
  a *freira* is its inmate. The Orthodox woman monastic is a **monja**.
- **Padroeiro** is allowed and *patrono* is not: the first is the ordinary
  Portuguese word for the saint of a place, the second is a Latinism that
  reads as legal.
- **Concílio** for a council of the Church, **sínodo** for a synod, and
  never *conclave*.
- **Grande Mártir**, not *megalomártir* and not *grão-mártir*. All three are
  met in Portuguese Orthodox printing; the first is what the Brazilian
  jurisdictions print most and is the one a reader will not stumble over.

Where Portuguese-speaking Orthodoxy has settled a word, that word is used.
This is not a field where the site invents.

afterwards.

## Where São, Santo and Santa may stand

This is the decision that touches the most lines of the vocabulary, because
the vocabulary is full of churches, monasteries, towns and dedications, and
every one of them wants the short form.

**Before a person named as a saint in his own right, the honorific is his
rank, not the bare word.** *O santo mártir Jorge*, not *São Jorge, o mártir*;
*nosso venerável pai Sérgio*, not *São Sérgio*. The vocabulary is what stands
beside a life on a card, so it speaks of the saint the way the life does.

**Before the name of a thing, the short form is the only right one.** A
church, a monastery, a town, a gate, a bridge, a feast that names itself
after a saint - these are proper names in Portuguese and they take *São*,
*Santo*, *Santa* and nothing else: *o mosteiro de São Sabas*, *a igreja de
Santa Irene*, *a lavra de Santo Atanásio*, *São Petersburgo*, *São Paulo*.
Writing *a igreja do santo mártir Jorge* for a dedication is the calendar's
language put where the map's language belongs.

**The Portuguese apocopation rule is not the Spanish one, and this is the
commonest way a Spanish crib leaks into Portuguese.** Spanish says *santo*
before *To-* and *Do-* alone; Portuguese says *Santo* before every masculine
name that begins with a vowel or an *h*:

- **São** before a masculine name beginning with a consonant: São João, São
  Pedro, São Nicolau, São Basílio, São Tomé, São Domingos.
- **Santo** before a masculine name beginning with a vowel or *h*: Santo
  André, Santo Antônio, Santo Estêvão, Santo Elias, Santo Inácio, Santo
  Agostinho. And **Santo** standing before a noun rather than a name: o
  Santo Sepulcro, o Santo Monte, os Lugares Santos, o Santo Sínodo.
- **Santa** before a feminine name, always: Santa Ana, Santa Tecla, Santa
  Teodora, Santa Irene.
- Capitalised where it is part of a proper name (São Sabas the monastery),
  lower case where it is the adjective doing its ordinary work (o santo
  mártir Jorge, os santos padres).

## The ranks the English writes as a compound

The calendar carries 175 distinct rank strings and most of the compound ones
are two ranks joined by a comma. Portuguese joins them with **e** where both
are borne at once and keeps the comma where the second names an office:
*Bishop, Hieromartyr* becomes **hieromártir e bispo**, because the
Portuguese ear puts the order of sanctity first and the see after it.
*Abbot (Igumen)* is **igumeno**, not a gloss inside brackets: Portuguese has
the word and does not need to explain the Greek to itself.

Where the English carries a parenthetical that is a real alternative name -
*(Voino-Yasenetsky)*, *(Abibus)* - the parenthesis is kept, because it is
the index's own apparatus and not a gloss.

## Spelling

The house text rules hold: hyphens and never dashes, straight quotes, one
blank line between paragraphs. To them Portuguese adds its own.

- **Accents are written in full, on capitals as well.** *Éfeso*, *África*,
  *Ávila*, *Ásia Menor*. A capital letter does not lose its accent; that was
  a typewriter's limitation and never a rule of the language.
- **The tilde is a letter's own mark and is never dropped**: *Dormição*,
  *São*, *coração*, *ão* and *ães* and *ões* in the plural. *Confissões*,
  *orações*, *irmãos*.
- **The cedilla likewise**: *Conceição*, *Ascensão*, *aparição*, *igreja de
  Santa Conceição*.
- Accented letters are written **precomposed**. An *a* followed by a
  combining tilde looks identical on the page and searches as a different
  word; `tools/loop.py` refuses the block.

## Cyrillic is transliterated the Portuguese way

Portuguese-language Orthodox books do not pass Slavic names through the
English, and they do not pass them through the Spanish either - the two
languages spell the same sounds differently and the differences are exactly
the letters these names are full of.

| Cyrillic | Portuguese | | Cyrillic | Portuguese |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| в | v | | х | kh |
| ж | j | | ц | ts |
| з | z | | ч | tch |
| ш | ch | | щ | sch |
| й | i | | ы | y |
| ю | iu | | э | e |
| я | ia | | ё | io |
| г | g, gu before e and i | | е after a vowel | ie |

So Сергий is **Sérgio**, Чернигов **Tchernígov**, Шуя **Chuia**, Жировицы
**Jirovitsy**, Хутынь **Khutyn**, Радонеж **Radonej**.

**An s between two vowels is written ss.** This is not decoration: a single
*s* between vowels is voiced in Portuguese, so *Vasili* is read *vaZILi* and
is not the name. **Vassíli**, **Anastássia**, **Odessa**, **Larissa**,
**Cassiano**.

**The written accent carries the Russian stress.** Portuguese stress is
fixed by the ending - a word ending in a consonant other than *m* or *s* is
stressed on its last syllable - so an unmarked *Novgorod* is read
*novgoRÓD* and an unmarked *Vladimir* *vladiMIR*, neither of which is a word
anyone says. The accent goes where the Portuguese rule would otherwise move
the stress off where the Russian puts it, and stays off where the two
already agree: **Nóvgorod**, **Vladímir**, **Súzdal**, **Vólogda**,
**Tchernígov**, **Riazan**, **Kostromá**, **Múrom**, **Óptina**, and plain
**Sarov**, **Smolensk**, **Kazan**, **Irkutsk**, **Tobolsk**, **Kiev**.

It is written on the names a reader will say aloud - the cities, the lands,
the great houses. The small northern foundations, whose names a Portuguese
reader meets once and does not say, keep the plain transliteration:
Belozersk, Borovsk, Dymsk, Galitch, Perekop, Solovki, Vorbozomsk.

Greek and Latin names take the Portuguese form where Portuguese has one -
**Éfeso**, **Ancira**, **Nicomédia**, **Cesareia**, **Calcedônia**,
**Tessalônica**, **Adrianópolis**, **Ásia Menor**, **Antioquia**,
**Alexandria**, **Constantinopla**, **Jerusalém**, **Esmirna**, **Trebizonda**
- and are otherwise transliterated from the Greek rather than passed through
the English: *Amaseia*, *Anazarbo*, *Arianzo*.

Celtic, English and Georgian names keep their own spelling, since Portuguese
has received no form for most of them, and take a Portuguese preposition
around it: *de Lindisfarne*, *de Iona*, *de Gareji*.

## What a script can catch in a Portuguese value

- **A Greek or Cyrillic letter.** Portuguese is written in Latin letters, so
  one Cyrillic character means a name was copied by eye from a Russian
  source and one letter was never touched. `tools/loop.py` refuses the
  block, and it fires before the file is written.
- **A combining mark.** See above; the tilde and the cedilla make this
  likelier in Portuguese than in any other language here.
- **A value identical to its English key** means nothing was written -
  except where it is a name Portuguese spells the same way, which is a real
  and large set.
- **A house-rule character**: a long dash, a curly quote, a soft hyphen.

What no script can test is whether the Portuguese reads as Portuguese, and
that is the whole of the work. The particular thing to listen for here is
the Spanish crib underneath: *hallazgo* to *hallazgo*-shaped Portuguese,
*santo Tomás* where Portuguese says *São Tomé*, *iglesia* to *igreja* by
reflex and *convento* along with it. Spanish is the nearest language and the
nearest language is the one that leaks.

## The fields, and what each one is

The vocabulary is not one kind of phrase, and knowing which field a phrase
came from settles most of the questions about how to render it.

| field | count | what it is | how Portuguese takes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| patronCauses | 2,622 | what is asked of him | a noun phrase, lower case, with the article Portuguese would use |
| patronWork | 1,572 | whom he is asked for | likewise |
| related | 1,476 | the kindred commemoration | a name and its apposition |
| icon | 1,407 | how he is written in an icon | a full descriptive sentence |
| titles | 980 | how else the saint is named | *de Ancira*, *o Confessor*, *bispo de Éfeso* |
| relics | 749 | where the relics rest | a full sentence, as the English is |
| place, origin, region, country | 1,197 | town, then the land it stood in | received form, comma as in the English |
| patronPlaces | 537 | the town a saint is patron of | the bare place name |
| rank, type, state, era, feastRank | 240 | the badge on a card | a noun phrase, capitalised as a label |
| canonizedBy | 145 | who glorified him and when | a clause: *Igreja da Rússia, glorificado em 1108* |
| baptismalName | 101 | the name before tonsure | the Portuguese form of the name |

The two largest are the intercessions and the icons, and they are the two
where the temptation to render word for word is strongest, because both are
English of a deliberately heightened kind. *The demons driven from the
temples* is **os demônios expulsos dos templos** and not a relative clause;
*A tall austere virgin martyr with a radiant crown* is **Uma virgem mártir
alta e austera, com uma coroa resplandecente**. Portuguese puts the
adjective after the noun and does not stack three of them in front of it.

## The order of work

| | where it is written | what publishes it | where it stands |
|---|---|---|---|
| the vocabulary | `tools/saint_terms/pt.py` | `tools/build_saint_terms.py` | in hand |
| the lives | `tools/saint_lives/pt.py` | `tools/build_saint_lives.py` | not begun |
| the calendar entries | `tools/saint_info/pt.py` | `tools/saint_info_i18n.py` | not begun |

Vocabulary first, and not by preference: `check_register.py --scaffold`
derives a language's rank patterns from its own terms table and refuses a
language that has not got one, so lives written before a vocabulary are
lives written without the check the vocabulary would have supplied. See
`docs/LOOP.md`.

The loop is one command:

```bash
python3 tools/loop.py terms pt --status
python3 tools/loop.py terms pt --next 40
python3 tools/loop.py terms pt --append batch.txt && \
  python3 tools/build_saint_terms.py --check && \
  python3 tools/loop.py terms pt --next 40
```

`tools/saint_terms/pt.py` is written entirely as `TEXT`. `PARTS` and
`expand()` are an optimisation the older languages took and are not
required; deciding per phrase whether it is an atom or a compound is a
judgment per phrase, and a judgment per phrase is a stop per phrase.

## The trap at the end

`data/saint-terms.v5.*.json` is the name the Saints page fetches, and it is
rewritten under that same name while being served `immutable,
max-age=31536000`. A reader who opens the Saints page in Portuguese while
the vocabulary is a tenth written holds that tenth for a year. So when
Portuguese is finished the filename version moves and the fetch in
`saints.html` moves with it.

Publishing is not this sitting's work. `--check` only; no `--write`, no
stamp, no page touched.
